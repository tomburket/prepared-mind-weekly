AI · Ops
Retail/CPG AI adoption hits 91% — and experienced operators urge starting with “boring use cases”
NVIDIA Blog · Cynthia Countouris · January 7, 2026
NVIDIA’s third-annual State of AI in Retail and CPG survey, drawing on hundreds of industry respondents, reports 91% of retail and CPG companies are actively using or assessing AI; 90% will increase AI budgets in 2026; and 89% credit AI with revenue increases, with 30% reporting lift greater than 10%. Agentic AI is moving from concept to deployment: 47% are using or assessing it, and 20% report AI agents already active in their organizations. Chris Walton, co-CEO of Omni Talk, on what’s actually working: “What executives should be focused on is not green-lighting vanity projects at the expense of high-ROI wins. The retailers who will succeed will start with boring use cases that solve specific P&L problems, prove the value, then scale.”
Why it matters
NVIDIA’s choice of voices is the read. Not generative-AI futurists but operator commentators urging discipline on “vanity projects” and “boring use cases.” A vendor selling the most expensive AI infrastructure in the industry is positioning around operator restraint. That’s a category-marketing move worth noticing, separate from whether the survey numbers themselves are accurate.

Ford Energy launches as wholly owned subsidiary, redirecting Kentucky EV battery capacity to data center storage
Fast Company · Taylor Hatmaker · May 28, 2026
Ford Motor Co. formally launched Ford Energy on May 11 as a wholly owned subsidiary that will manufacture battery energy storage systems for utilities, data centers and large industrial customers from a repurposed Glendale, Kentucky facility. The subsidiary targets at least 20 GWh in annual deployment with first deliveries in late 2027; Lisa Drake is president. Ford’s stock is up more than 30% in the weeks since launch, hitting highs the automaker hasn’t seen since 2022. The batteries rely on lithium iron phosphate technology licensed from CATL, the Chinese manufacturer; Rep. John Moolenaar, R-Mich., has pressed CEO Jim Farley on the national-security implications of Chinese technology powering critical US infrastructure.
Why it matters
The capacity Ford built for EVs is being redirected to demand that exists — AI data center storage — without waiting for the original market to return. The political pressure on Chinese-licensed technology powering critical US infrastructure is the open question.

As channels converged, the CCO role moved up the C-suite — and the salaries followed
WSJ CMO Today · Katie Deighton · June 8, 2026
Per Korn Ferry data cited in WSJ: nearly half of CCOs report directly to the CEO in 2025, up from 37% in 2015. Median base $400K–$450K, up 12–14% in two years; one in seven now earn $2 million-plus, compared with 2% in the top bracket a decade ago. AB InBev appointed its first CCO after the Bud Light boycott, moving the role from legal to CEO direct-report. Goodyear’s Travis Parman: “I’ll refer to myself as the Chief Truth Teller, just so I can be really candid.”
Why it matters
Deighton’s argument: comms moved from press-release writer to consigliere because channels converged — investor memos, ad copy, social, and LLM results all draw from the same narrative, and the cost of misalignment has risen. Half of comms leaders are now tasked with leading or partnering on their organization’s AI change management.

Liaquat Ahamed: the AI capex boom’s funding structure has shifted in ways that echo the 1870s railroad bonds
Fortune · Liaquat Ahamed · June 2, 2026
Liaquat Ahamed, Pulitzer-winning author of “Lords of Finance” and the newly-released “1873” (Penguin Press, June 2), argues in a Fortune commentary that current AI infrastructure investment — projected at $800 billion to $1 trillion per year, 2–3% of GDP — has structural similarity to the 1870s railroad construction boom, which ran at roughly 2.5% of GDP. He identifies two recent shifts that have made the funding structure more vulnerable: competitive pressure pushing hyperscalers from internal funds toward external debt, private credit, and securitized-debt special-purpose vehicles; and the war in the Middle East tightening global equity and credit markets and likely slowing Middle East capital supply. Ahamed’s central historical example: banker Jay Cooke committed in the early 1870s to raising $100 million for the Northern Pacific Railroad — equivalent to over $1 trillion today relative to the global economy — and began using his own bank’s depositor funds when public bond sales fell short. On September 18, 1873, Cooke’s creditors closed the bank. The NYSE suspended trading for ten days. Within two years, a third of all railroad bonds in the country had stopped paying interest.
Why it matters
Ahamed closes the piece on two points. The first: “the line between visionary investment and ruinous excess is invisible until after you have crossed it. We may have already crossed it.” The second: 1873 set back the development of the U.S. railroad network by half a decade, but the railroads survived to become the central vehicle of American economic growth for the next fifty years. Ahamed expects the AI capital investment boom, even if it suffers a temporary setback, will be with us for decades.

Refereeing becomes a real-time data system at the 2026 World Cup
Fox Sports · Doug McIntyre · October 2, 2025 & Open Magazine · June 2026
The Adidas Trionda match ball for the 2026 FIFA World Cup carries a 500 Hz inertial measurement unit sensor — capturing motion data 500 times per second and transmitting in real time to the Video Assistant Referee. Hannes Schaefke, Adidas Football Innovation Lead, on the architecture: “We can track every event, every interaction the player has with the ball. Every location at any given moment is being tracked by a local positioning system.” Optical-tracking cameras at each host stadium follow 29 anatomical points per player 50 times per second, integrated with the ball-sensor timestamp to support semi-automated offside decisions. FIFA and technology partner Lenovo have created 3D AI avatars of all 1,248 tournament players from one-second body scans, used to support offside reconstruction and broadcast replays. The audio-alert threshold for routing offside calls directly to the assistant referee has tightened from 50 cm (tested at the Club World Cup) to 10 cm for 2026.
Why it matters
A sporting event has been instrumented at the data-collection density of a manufacturing operation — 500 Hz ball, 3D scans of all 1,248 players, offside audio alerts at 10 cm. The match ball is no longer just a ball; it is a measurement device with a battery that requires charging before kickoff. The structural fact is that real-time human judgment is being progressively replaced by real-time data-decision systems — in a category that, until recently, was treated as inherently analog.

Cave art logistics imply the deep-time origins of hierarchy — Garate’s Basque field campaign
The Guardian · Stephen Phelan · June 2, 2026
Stephen Phelan, in a Guardian long read, travels northern Spain with Diego Garate Maidagan, professor of prehistory and paleolithic art at the University of Cantabria, through the painted caves at Altamira, Atxurra and across the Basque Country. Garate has spent his career deputizing Basque cavers to spot the faint pigment traces and engravings that earlier surveys missed, revealing that what we thought was the cave-art record is a small visible fraction of what was actually there. His closing observation: the logistical complexity of producing this art, with expeditions deep underground carrying paint, charcoal and fat-burning lamps, and trained artisans working in teams while others handled survival tasks, “implies the creation of surpluses in some kind of hierarchy.” It may point, he suggests, to the very origins of human inequality.
Why it matters
The same logistical complexity visible in any modern operating organization, surpluses and specialization, has a deep-time precedent in Palaeolithic art expeditions. Garate’s argument is that this kind of organization is what stratifies a society. Logistics implies surplus; surplus implies hierarchy; hierarchy implies inequality.

“Love my woke pope”: the first AI encyclical crosses into mass culture
Associated Press · Krysta Fauria · June 2, 2026
Pope Leo XIV released his first encyclical, Magnifica Humanitas, on May 25, presenting it at the Vatican alongside Anthropic co-founder Christopher Olah, a decision some Catholic critics publicly contested. The 42,300-word document calls for robust regulation of artificial intelligence and warns against autonomous weapons, concentrated technological power and the erosion of human dignity. The AP’s story is the reception: the Instagram meme account Saint Hoax posted a video on the pope’s call to disarm AI to its more than 3 million followers, captioned “Love my woke pope (I’m not even Catholic).” Boston comedian Isabel Thurston’s video of two friends studying printed pages of the encyclical over margaritas passed 3 million views. Robert Orsi, professor of religious studies and history at Northwestern University, reads the document as distinctly American, with language that recalls the Declaration of Independence.
Why it matters
The AP traces the enthusiasm, especially among young people, to a perception that few political or global leaders take AI’s ramifications seriously, and that most have accommodated the tech industry in the name of growth. A 42,300-word teaching document from a 2,000-year-old institution going viral on Instagram suggests the audience for serious institutional positioning on AI is much broader, and much younger, than typically assumed.

FIFA’s India broadcast deal closes 10 days before kickoff at less than half the asking price
Reuters · June 1, 2026 · via Al Jazeera & Variety
FIFA closed India broadcast rights with Zee Entertainment on June 1, 10 days before the tournament’s June 11 kickoff. Reuters reported FIFA initially sought $100 million for the 2026 and 2030 World Cups combined, then cut the ask to $60 million; the Economic Times reports the final Zee deal exceeds $40 million for a 39-event package through 2034. Viacom18, JioStar’s predecessor, paid roughly $60 million for Qatar 2022 alone — when time zones favored Indian audiences. FIFA chief business officer Romy Gai’s statement: “The Indian market is of strategic importance for FIFA as it displays immense potential driven by a young and passionate audience.” China’s rights closed two weeks earlier; China Media Group secured a comprehensive package on May 15, ending what Reuters called a “standoff.”
Why it matters
The external message is record interest and broadest reach in tournament history. The closed-deal record runs the other way: less per event than Viacom18 paid for Qatar 2022 alone — when Indian audiences could watch in daylight. A structural constraint, match times, set the price ceiling. The global brand absorbed the difference.
