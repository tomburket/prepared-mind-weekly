Board · Governance
A study found every salad dressing labeled avocado oil contained other oils. Texas opened an investigation.
Food Dive · August 27, 2026
Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton opened an investigation into PepsiCo's Siete Foods, Kraft Heinz-owned Primal Kitchen and mayonnaise and salad dressing manufacturer Chosen Foods over claims their products are made with avocado oil. The inquiry follows a University of California, Davis study finding that 89 percent of processed foods claiming to be made with avocado oil contained other oils, including 93 percent of chips, 71 percent of mayonnaises and 100 percent of the salad dressings tested. Primal Kitchen said it sources "our avocado oil from trusted suppliers who confirm the oil's integrity through independent, third-party laboratory testing." The UC Davis percentages are study findings; the state has opened an inquiry and adjudicated nothing.
Why it matters
The gap under investigation is not between a label and a regulation. It is between a label and a supply chain the brand does not own, verified by the third-party attestation that most food companies rely on and that the study went around.

02AI & Ops
AI · Ops
Instacart says one prompt at checkout, asking whether you forgot something, lifts sales nearly 1 percent
Forbes · August 21, 2026
Instacart says thousands of its Caper Carts, shopping carts with built-in cameras and screens that identify items as shoppers load them, are now live across more than 100 cities, with that business tripling year over year. David McIntosh, the company's chief connected stores officer, said a personalized "Did you forget?" prompt shown as shoppers approach checkout drove nearly a 1 percent absolute sales lift. Around 600,000 Instacart shoppers can capture shelf imagery using their phones, feeding on-shelf conditions back to a company that has processed more than 1.6 billion lifetime orders. McIntosh also said more than 80 percent of North American shoppers are shopping to a budget. Every figure here is Instacart's own, relayed by one reporter, with no supporting data released.
Why it matters
A 1 percent absolute lift across grocery volume is a large number reached by a small mechanism: adding one item to the basket of a shopper the same executive describes as working to a budget. Whether that is service or friction depends on who is measuring, and the only party measuring is the one selling the cart.

Sprouts moved fresh meat into its own distribution centers. Now the network decides where the stores go.
Supply Chain Dive · August 19, 2026
Around 85 percent of Sprouts Farmers Market stores now carry fresh meat that flows through the company's own distribution centers rather than through outside wholesalers. The chain plans new stores within a 250-mile radius of a distribution center, and is on track to open more than 40 locations this year. In four target cities, a company executive said Sprouts aims to have around 35 stores operating by 2030, enough to warrant planning a distribution center for the region. The 2030 store count is described as an aim rather than a commitment.
Why it matters
Self-distribution is usually argued on cost per case. Here it is setting real estate strategy, with the store footprint drawn to fill the network rather than the network built to chase the footprint.

A $138 million beef loss, two plants closed, a third for sale, and 2,500 union jobs gone at Joslin
Food Dive · August 14, 2026
Tyson Foods will close its beef plant in Joslin, Illinois, which the company said will lead to the layoffs of around 2,500 union workers in the region, and will also close a facility in Eagle Mountain, Utah, that Tyson previously said was capable of employing between 800 and 1,200 people. The company is pursuing a sale of a third beef plant, in Pasco, Washington. Production consolidates into three central U.S. locations: Dakota City, Nebraska; Holcomb, Kansas; and Amarillo, Texas. Tyson's beef segment reported an operating loss of $138 million. In a statement, the company said the consolidation will help it "create a more competitive footprint amidst one of the most historic cattle shortages the country has ever experienced"; chief executive Donnie King told an earnings call the move "will not solve the entire gap of beef losses we are currently seeing."
Why it matters
The company's framing puts the cause upstream in the cattle supply, while the cost lands in three towns and one segment's operating line. By the chief executive's own account, the consolidation does not close the gap it was built to close.

The Workforce
Minimum wage increases cut employment, hours and earnings for low-wage workers in poor families, a new paper finds
NBER · August 2026
David Neumark and Emma Wohl report what they describe as "the first direct estimates of the effects of minimum wages on low-wage workers in families at different points of the distribution of income-to-needs." Drawing on the Survey of Income and Program Participation, which oversamples low-income families, they find "adverse - rather than beneficial - effects of minimum wages on the employment, hours, and earnings of initially-employed low-wage workers in poor and low-income families." The authors report no gradient, meaning the poorest low-wage workers were not affected more severely than others in the low-income group, and argue the adverse effects "help explain why minimum wages do not reduce poverty." The paper is an NBER working paper and has not been peer reviewed; Neumark has published research skeptical of minimum wage increases for roughly three decades.
Why it matters
The finding lands on exactly the workers the policy names, using survey data built to find them. It is also one working paper from a researcher whose priors are on the record, in a literature that has not converged in thirty years, which is the condition under which operators set wage floors anyway.

Heritage Grocers filled five C-suite seats at once, including a chief logistics officer it did not have
Grocery Dive · August 27, 2026
Heritage Grocers Group, which operates four grocery banners including Cardenas Markets, Los Altos Ranch Market and El Rancho Supermercado, announced five C-suite appointments on Wednesday, among them a chief logistics officer in a role the company created. Adrian Castaneda was promoted into the logistics seat, alongside internal promotions for Vince Gambino as chief merchandising officer and Adam Salgado as chief experience officer. The company hired Maria McDonald as chief people officer and Gil Salazar as chief information officer from outside, and named Ryan Adams and Jay Sharrock as division presidents. Heritage said the changes reflect efforts to strengthen its operating model and meet the "unique needs" of each of its banners.
Why it matters
Five seats filled in a single announcement is not a succession, it is a rebuild. The seat that did not previously exist is the one that moves freight, which is a statement about where a four-banner operator thinks its next margin is.

A Panama Canal slot averaged $55,000 before February. One just sold for $5.3 million.
gCaptain · August 25, 2026
SK Gas Ltd., a South Korea-based shipper, won the auction to transit the Panama Canal on September 1 aboard the liquefied petroleum gas tanker G. Spirit, paying $5.3 million. The price surpassed a previous high of $4.6 million set earlier the same month, also paid by a South Korean vessel operator, making it the second record in August. Median auction prices before February averaged around $55,000. Vessels that do not reserve a slot are waiting 11 days. The Canal Authority said the results reflect "significantly stronger demand."
Why it matters
The auction is the only part of the canal that moves on price, so it absorbs all of the scarcity the queue creates. What a slot averaged in January and what one shipper paid to skip 11 days are the same commodity priced by different mechanisms.

A decade of headlines promised humans on Mars by 2026. No rocket has gone, crewed or uncrewed.
Defector · August 27, 2026
Sabrina Imbler catalogued the predictions as their deadline year arrived: the Wall Street Journal in 2011, "Elon Musk: We Can Put A Man On Mars In 10 Years"; Business Insider in 2016 on a plan "to begin colonizing Mars by 2022"; the Los Angeles Times the same year, "SpaceX could send people to Mars in 2024"; CNET, "We'll have people on Mars by 2025"; National Geographic in 2017, "In Seven Years, SpaceX Could Land Humans on Mars." As Imbler writes, "SpaceX has not sent any rockets to Mars, crewed or uncrewed." An earlier venture, Mars One, drew serious coverage from the Guardian, the Washington Post and CNN after MIT researchers calculated that "the first Martian settlers would suffocate and die within the first 68 days of landing on the red planet"; it "dissolved in bankruptcy in 2019." Musk has since pivoted to the Moon on a timeline of less than 10 years. Earlier this month a stray SpaceX rocket segment, off-course for 19 months, crashed into the lunar surface.
Why it matters
Each of those headlines accurately reported what a named source said on a named date. None of the dates held, and the forecasts expired one at a time in outlets that had no mechanism for noticing.

Oddly Interesting
An 1,800-year-old shield boss turned up in a Polish scrap yard, still packed with burnt bone
HeritageDaily · August 23, 2026
An iron shield boss, the domed central fitting that shields the hand of whoever carries the shield, was recovered from a scrap yard in Hrubieszow, in eastern Poland. Inside it was dried soil containing burnt bone fragments and crop stubble. The finder, Wlodzimierz Starykiewicz, chose not to remove the material, leaving it intact for museum analysis. Researchers date the piece to roughly the 2nd century AD and theorize it belonged to a warrior of the Przeworsk culture, associated in this context with Vandal communities; heat marks on the iron are consistent with placement near a funeral pyre.
Why it matters
The fitting survived eighteen centuries, a scrap yard, and the ordinary impulse to tip out the dirt. What is inside it has not been analyzed yet.
